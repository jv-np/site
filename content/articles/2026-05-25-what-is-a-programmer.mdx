This question, hmmm, it's interesting right?

A programmer is...

In the age of AI, a programmer is...

In the future, a programmer is...

Is a "vibe coder" a programmer...?

Programming is doing, right? You may only identify a carpenter because he may practice carpentry.

You can only identify a doctor because he may act as a doctor.

Circular? No, it's a recursive definition. We already know the activities associated with "acting as a doctor", so the definition works by invoking said meaning. So we can go from that to:

> A doctor is someone who may treat someone who is suffering using medicine knowledge, he may respect life, he may abide by the Hipocrates vow and he may chose life over death whenever possible.

So what is a programmer? Rather, what a programmer does?

Well, for one, he writes code.

The mere idea of "writing code" seems like an offense for any of the most sharp "vibe coders" out there. "Why we need to do this now the AI can do it for us?" and they would be... correct? No, I don't think this is the proper word, rather it would seem reasonable in the surface of them to question that. However the point about writing code is not so much about the direct and physical act of smashing a keyboard's individual letters to produce a specific pattern, thinking like this would be very... naive, right? Because there are all sorts of ways you could write code, if you think about it.

If you suddenly go blind, let's say it happens, and you are someone who rely on the visual feedback of your screen to type code, and now you are doing it by voice using some assistive technology... are you less of a programmer than I am as I write code letter-by-letter using my hands? I don't think so.

But now, obviously the spoken language is very hard to coerce into a syntactically rigid structure right? And even more if you are not used to it since your infancy. What I mean is, when you speak there are a lot of biases that exist, you could spell the wrong thing, or pronounce a keyword wrongly, and navigating through the code would be... annoying to say the least, so you can see why this would be the smoothest of the experiences. So, suppose we have a very nice technology, a database that can take more loose queries and produce matching code snippets. Simple code snippets, like "x = y" or "function_call(...)", or even some conditional structures. It hears your voice, transcribes it, then an NLP algorithm extracts to the best of it's abilities information from what you said. It takes that processed information and smartly converts it into a nice template on your screen, so now your job is easier, you just need to fill the gaps. This still would not be less called "writing code" as when you do by hand, right?

And... let's expand this reasoning a little bit, maybe a lot because I don't want to waste your time and the reasoning is very incremental². Suppose AI is now fast enough to at least write the exact functions you describe to it for you, using your voice. Well this seems great, instead of a database writing small snippets and you needing to compose them manually, you can now describe in looser terms what you want the machine to formalize into code, and it will do so, it's much more efficient when you think about it, specially for someone blind, right? Of course, this assumes the results will be average, but not absurdly terrible to the point you would need to fix everything with manual instructions again, and in the cases it messes up it should be less total work to do a small precise fix manually, or to simply regenerate the results. So, in this case, is you less of a programmer than me or anyone that writes code by hand, letter-by-letter? Or lessen than someone that's blind and uses the last assistive technology? Do we have an hierarchy of who is "more of a programmer" or "who that writes more code" based on who is doing the most manual effort?

I believe those questions should harbor an interesting conclusion to the reasonable reader, and I'll not pretend everyone will agree with said conclusion. But to spoil those who could not infer this: the answer is that... well no, probably? Because writing code... writing... writing seems very mechanical if you try to decompose it into the physical action of typing letters, but it's not so much mechanical the moment you abstract away things that seem contingent. So the blind programmer using an AI to generate functions does not seem to be less of a programmer than the one using the assistive technology to generate snippets, and this one does not seem to be less of a programmer than the one that "types" directly using his voice. And neither of them seem to be less of a programmer than those who write using their hands.  But what this has to do with my earlier provocation about what it means to write code?

Well, when you think about it, this is all vanity. That because "I'm using my hands, and I'm having a hard time doing things", this implies that "I'm doing something worth doing."

But is it? If we accept that the blind programmer using AI to generate functions is no less of a programmer, and it's still fundamentally writing code, then what this tells us about non-blind programmers who use AI to write their code? It seems to be, trying to make a distinction here would be pointless once you see that progression.

Using your voice, using the snippets feature of your IDE, using traditional autocomplete, relying on AI autocomplete, using AI to write a function, using AI to write a file, using AI to write to many files... I can't see the point where I stop calling this act programming<FootnoteRef id="1" />.

I believe the best "cutoff point" for this is, ironically, how much you see AI as a tool rather than as a being. Or rather, to be more precise, how much you see that as a means to an end rather than something that you are merely managing and has it's own will.

What do I mean? Well it's simple, when something is a means to and end for you using that implies that, essentially, you are the one doing the action. The phrase "using an assistive technology to write" already contains what I mean, "using", it's the act of taking something and employing it in the fulfillment of a goal. It does not matter if you are using your hands, your voice or even your mind, in the end what matters is that you are the one in control in the end of the loop.

Why is that? Think about it, we already give names to roles based on what they do. If you manage a software and the team building it, we call you a "tech lead". If you indirectly manage software using people to work for you, we call you a "manager". If a manager reports to someone, that someone may as well be a director. Each role composes on top of the other, but all of them are still fundamentally complimentary in a sense.

So, as you see the AI less as a tool and more as "something you just manage", you slowly lose your identity as a programmer and becomes something else. And I'm not sure doing this is the right step, you may be trying to fit too many pigeons in too little holes.

But then... we know what is writing code, but what is code?

Code is the written form of algorithms, and algorithms are <LinkRef id="algorithm-definition">description of a pattern of behaviour of primitive possible actions.</LinkRef> That's about it.

<LinkPreview id="algorithm-definition">
  <Preview>
  <author>Dijkstra</author>
  An algorithm is the description of a pattern of behaviour, expressed in terms of a well-understood, finite repertoire of named (so-called "primitive") actions of which it is assumed a priori that they can be done (i.e. can be caused to happen).
  </Preview>
  <To src="https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~EWD/transcriptions/EWD03xx/EWD316.2.html#:~:text=An%20algorithm,happen%29%2E" />
</LinkPreview>

And programs, of course, are the amalgamation of code in order to produce a specific pattern of behavior in a machine. Every single layer is composed from the last.

So, a programmer writes code, a programmer writes code. This is a very important part of being a programmer, it seems.

So when we go back to the questions we posed... are programmers useless in the age of AI? For anyone paying attention, the answer should be very obvious that it is a resounding no.

Because we may have less friction now writing code than before, allegedly, but we are still the ones directing those systems, we are still the ones designing them and trying to be precise about the computations we want to happen. <LinkRef id="programmer-responsibility">Our understanding nor the responsibilities we bear as programmers are not things that can be replaced, nor it's wise to think that.</LinkRef>

<LinkPreview id="programmer-responsibility">
  <Preview>
  <author>Dijkstra</author>
  Secondly, if his creations are going to be relied upon, it will be the programmer's primary task to design his artifacts so understandable, that he can take the responsibility for them, and, regardless of the answer to the question how much of his current activity may ultimately be delegated to machines, we should always remember that neither "understanding" nor "being responsible" can properly be classified as activities: they are more like "states of mind" and are intrinsically incapable of being delegated.
  </Preview>
  <To src="https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~EWD/transcriptions/EWD05xx/EWD540.html#:~:text=Secondly,delegated,-%2E" />
</LinkPreview>

Which also responds to one of our original questions. Is a vibe coder a programmer? I would rather say, the vibe coder is an aberration. It's someone that denies the very own principles of understanding and responsibility that exists in being a programmer. Therefore, the answer should be a resounding NO<FootnoteRef id="2" />.

If you understand what you are doing and the code you are producing, then you could by no means call your act "vibe coding". The mere act of using AI to write code, even if you have less absolute control and precision as you would have by writing the code manually, should not be called "vibe coding", this kind of conflation can only cause harm.

This also does not imply vibe coding is something inherently bad, or that you are less of a programmer if you practice some "vibe code", it's the consistent abandonment of your own understanding of what you build as an engineer, the distancing of yourself from what you make, that degrades you as a programmer, ***abusus non tollit usum***.

Thus, in the age of AI, what is a programmer? It's still, fundamentally, someone who writes code.
And in the future, a programmer is... still, fundamentally, someone who writes code<FootnoteRef id="3" />.

---

Talking about this may feel tiresome, but I believe it's rather important. Programming is not dead, contrary to what some top figure in the AI industry may want you to think.
In truth, it will never be dead as long as someone is able to see through the clouds, as long as someone is still doing it, either by hand or with an assistive technology, or even using an AI as a tool<FootnoteRef id="0" />.
